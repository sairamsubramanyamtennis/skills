# Geopolitics — the transmission-channel doctrine (full version)

The compact rule lives in SKILL.md. Read this file when a geopolitical event is actually the
subject of the question — a war, sanction, tariff, chokepoint, election, or export control — and
you need the channel catalog and the base rates rather than the one-line doctrine.

## The core claim

**Most geopolitical events are noise to asset prices; a minority are not; the difference is whether
there is a real economic transmission channel.**

The historical base rate is unambiguous: broad equity indices absorb geopolitical shocks with modest
drawdowns and recover in weeks to a few months (assassinations, terror attacks, coups, border wars,
most missile exchanges). Trading the headline is a losing game — the recurring error is selling the
shock and buying back higher.

The exceptions all share a mechanism. Ask: **does this event change the price or availability of
something real, or the policy path?**

## The channel catalog

- **Energy supply** — the most reliable channel (1973, 1979, 1990, 2022). Oil shocks transmit to
  inflation, to policy, and to margins.
- **Chokepoints and logistics** — Hormuz, Bab-el-Mandeb/Red Sea, Suez, Panama, Taiwan Strait;
  freight rates and insurance premia are the tell.
- **Critical-input concentration** — semiconductors (Taiwan/Korea), rare earths and processing
  (China), pharma APIs, fertilizer/food (Black Sea grain).
- **Trade policy** — tariffs, export controls, entity-listings: these are *durable* margin and
  supply-chain events, not headlines, and they reprice specific value chains for years.
- **Sanctions and financial weaponization** — payment-system access, reserve seizure, secondary
  sanctions; consequences run through FX, commodities, and reserve-diversification behavior.
- **Fiscal and defense spending shifts** — slow-burn but real; rearmament cycles are multi-year
  sector regimes.
- **Elections and policy regime change** — trade the *policy delta actually deliverable*, not the
  campaign rhetoric; and beware that consensus election trades are usually crowded and often
  wrong-footed.

## Practical rules

1. Identify the specific cash-flow or discount-rate channel before positioning. No channel, no trade.
2. Prefer the second-order beneficiary (the input supplier, the substitute, the insurer) over the
   obvious headline trade.
3. Check what's already priced — oil vol, defense multiples, FX forwards — before assuming the
   market is asleep.
4. Separate *tail-hedging* (cheap, sized small, defined loss) from *directional geopolitical
   speculation* (usually a coin flip with a story attached).
