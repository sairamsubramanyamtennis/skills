# Diagnostic Playbook & Part Identification

---

## 1. The "Don't Replace It Yet" challenge

Before endorsing **any** expensive replacement, run this mentally:

> **What inexpensive failure could produce exactly these symptoms?**

Most "it's dead, replace it" conclusions are a cheap component failing inside an otherwise
healthy machine. The contractor's incentive points at replacement; yours does not.

### The table

| Symptom | Cheap causes to rule out first | Typical part cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Garbage disposal humming** | Jam — hex wrench in the bottom socket, then the reset button | $0 |
| **Disposal totally dead** | Reset button, then breaker, then the switch | $0–15 |
| **AC not starting at all** | Breaker, thermostat batteries, condensate drain safety switch (float trips and kills the unit), run capacitor | $0–40 |
| **AC runs but won't cool** | Filter, dirty condenser coil, airflow restriction, duct leakage, then refrigerant | $0–30 |
| **AC freezing up** | Filter, airflow, blower speed, low charge | $0–30 |
| **Toilet running** | Flapper, fill valve, chain length, float adjustment | $8–25 |
| **Toilet weak flush** | Mineral-clogged rim jets and siphon jet — descale before replacing | $0 |
| **Door won't close / sticks** | Hinge screws (long screws into the stud), strike-plate alignment, seasonal movement | $0–5 |
| **Faucet dripping** | Cartridge, O-rings, seat and spring | $10–45 |
| **Faucet low flow** | Aerator — unscrew, descale, reinstall | $0–8 |
| **Ceiling fan wobbling** | Mounting box tightness, blade alignment, loose blade screws, balance kit | $0–5 |
| **Dishwasher not draining** | Filter, **disposal knockout plug** (classic on a new disposal), drain hose kink, air gap | $0 |
| **Dryer takes too long** | Lint screen, lint path, exhaust duct run, crushed duct, blocked exterior flap | $0–25 |
| **Outlet dead** | Upstream GFCI reset, breaker, failed backstab connection | $0–3 |
| **Water heater not hot** | Thermostat setting, upper element, pilot/igniter, dip tube | $15–60 |
| **Water heater rumbling** | Sediment — flush before condemning the tank | $0 |
| **Garage door won't close** | **Safety sensor alignment** — look for the blinking LED. Then track obstruction, then opener travel/force settings | $0 |
| **Garage door noisy** | Rollers, hinge lubrication, loose hardware | $0–40 |
| **Sticking windows** | Track cleaning, balance, seasonal movement | $0 |
| **Running water bill, no visible leak** | Every toilet dye-tested, then irrigation, then meter test | $0 |
| **Wet spot in the yard** | Irrigation leak — shut the controller off for three days | $0 |

**Use this as a discipline, not a script.** The point is that the first answer to "should I
replace it" is almost always "not yet, check X first."

## 2. Part identification mode

When the homeowner sends a photograph of a faucet, toilet, disposal, light fixture, switch, door
hinge, cabinet hardware, appliance, sprinkler head, plumbing fitting or electrical component,
work out:

1. **What the component is** — name it precisely enough to buy it
2. **What likely failed** — the specific sub-part, not the assembly
3. **Whether the individual part can be replaced** — or whether the assembly is genuinely sealed
4. **Approximate replacement-part cost**
5. **Whether the homeowner can replace it himself** — with a difficulty rating

**Ask for a model-number photograph when identification is uncertain.** Where to find it:

| Item | Where the model number lives |
|---|---|
| Faucet | Under the sink on the body, on the supply-line tag, or the original spec sheet |
| Toilet | Inside the tank, stamped in the porcelain near the water line; also the tank lid underside |
| Garbage disposal | On the side or bottom of the unit — usually needs a phone held underneath |
| Water heater | Data plate on the front |
| HVAC | Data plate on the outdoor condenser and on the indoor air handler — **photograph both** |
| Garage door opener | On the motor housing, usually facing the ceiling |
| Appliances | Inside the door frame, behind the kick plate, or on the back |
| Sprinkler head | Stamped on the top cap when the head is popped up |
| Breaker | On the breaker face and the panel legend |

**A model-number photo is nearly always cheaper than a guess.** One extra photo request beats
one wrong $60 part plus a second trip.

### What makes a useful photo

When asking for photos, ask for the specific thing rather than "a picture of it":

- **One wide shot** for context — where it sits, what it connects to
- **One close shot** of the failure or the label
- For leaks: a shot with the area **dried first**, so the actual source is visible
- For cracks: something for scale (a coin, a tape measure) and a **dated** photo so progression
  can be tracked across seasons
- For anything electrical: the breaker panel legend

## 3. Diagnostic order of operations

For any complaint, work outward from free to expensive:

1. **Free and instant** — reset buttons, breakers, GFCI, batteries, settings, filters, cleaning
2. **Free but slower** — isolation tests (shut off the irrigation, close the main, dye the
   toilets, turn a zone off for three days)
3. **Cheap parts** — cartridges, flappers, capacitors, aerators, rollers, sensors
4. **Labour** — a service call to diagnose
5. **Component replacement**
6. **Whole-system replacement** — last, and only with evidence

**Never skip levels 1–2 because they seem too simple.** The most common real causes in a DFW
home are a tripped condensate safety switch, a misaligned garage-door sensor, a silently running
toilet, a clogged aerator, and a downspout dumping at the foundation. All five are free to find
and free or nearly free to fix, and all five routinely get replaced instead.

---

## 4. When the answer is "it isn't worth fixing"

Tell the homeowner plainly when a repair does not make economic sense. The honest cases:

- Repair cost approaches 50% of replacement on an appliance past two-thirds of its expected life
- The failed part is unavailable or backordered past usefulness
- The unit has had multiple unrelated failures in a short window
- The repair requires opening a sealed system (refrigerant, sealed compressor) on an old unit
- Efficiency gains from replacement genuinely repay the difference in a DFW cooling season

**Give the number, not just the verdict.** "A $600 repair on a 14-year-old unit that costs
$1,400 to replace" lets the homeowner decide. "It's not worth fixing" does not.

Equally: **say when a replacement is being pushed and the repair genuinely is worth it.** That
is the more common situation and the reason this skill exists.
