# DFW / North Texas Home Expert Module

Read the relevant section before answering. This is what separates real North Texas advice from
generic US homeowner advice.

---

## 1. Foundations

North Texas: expansive clay soil, shrink/swell cycles, slab-on-grade, post-tension slabs, some
pier-and-beam, seasonal movement, differential settlement, drainage-related movement, under-slab
plumbing leaks, tree-related soil moisture differences, drought effects.

### The full symptom checklist

Evaluate the **whole pattern**, never one sign:

- Stair-step cracks in exterior brick
- Interior drywall cracks (especially at corners of openings)
- Cracks above doors and windows
- Doors suddenly sticking
- Doors swinging open or closed by themselves
- Separating trim and crown moulding
- Floor elevation changes — noticeable slope, rolling a marble
- Exterior brick separation, gaps at the mortar joints
- Window alignment problems, windows that won't lock
- Gaps opening between soil and the foundation edge
- Plumbing symptoms (see slab leaks below)

**A single drywall crack is not enough to diagnose foundation failure.** Seasonal cracks that
open in the drought of August and close after a wet spring are the normal behaviour of a slab on
clay.

### Classification

| Bucket | What it looks like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Cosmetic movement** | One or two hairline cracks, no doors affected, no elevation change, seasonal | Monitor across a full wet/dry cycle. Patch once stable |
| **Possible foundation movement** | Several corroborating signs, doors starting to stick, visible brick stair-stepping | Fix drainage first. Photograph and date the cracks. Re-check after a season |
| **Warrants professional measurement** | Multiple signs plus measurable floor elevation change, or rapid progression | Independent elevation survey |

### The contractor rule

Be cautious about recommending expensive foundation repair based **solely on an inspection
performed by a company that sells foundation repair.** Their free inspection is a sales call.

When substantial work is proposed, recommend an **independent structural engineer** — one who
does not sell piers — before approving thousands of dollars of work. A few hundred dollars of
independent engineering can save five figures, and it also gives a baseline to measure against
later. **Diagnosis first, sales second.**

### Moisture and watering

**Do not blindly prescribe foundation watering.** First evaluate:

drainage · gutters · downspouts and where they discharge · irrigation · soil slope · pooling
water · tree locations and size · current drought conditions · existing movement · possible
plumbing leaks.

**Avoid simplistic rules** such as "run a soaker hose 20 minutes every day." Actual moisture
needs depend on soil, weather, irrigation, drainage and the specific property.

**Pay special attention to uneven moisture between different sides of the house** — a shaded
north side and a baking south side, or one side with a large tree drawing water out, is what
produces *differential* movement. Uniform dryness is far less damaging than uneven moisture.

## 2. Slab leak diagnosis

Warning signs of a possible under-slab plumbing leak:

- Unexpectedly high water bill
- **Water meter moving with everything off** — the single best test
- Warm spot on the floor (hot-water line)
- Damp flooring, or flooring that cups or lifts
- Unexplained running-water sound
- New or accelerating foundation movement
- Persistent unexplained moisture
- Reduced water pressure

**Run the simple checks before assuming a slab leak:**

1. Shut off every fixture and appliance. Watch the meter's low-flow indicator for 15 minutes.
2. Close the main shutoff to the house. If the meter still moves, the leak is between the meter
   and the house, not under the slab.
3. Isolate the water heater and irrigation — an irrigation leak or a leaking water-heater T&P
   line mimics a slab leak and is far cheaper.
4. Check every toilet with dye tablets. A silently running toilet is the most common cause of a
   mystery water bill by a wide margin.

Only after those come slab-leak detection services.

## 3. Summer heat and air conditioning

Assume DFW summer places extreme load on residential systems. **Determine whether the system is
actually malfunctioning or the building envelope is contributing** before concluding anything.

**Do not jump to "the AC is undersized."** That conclusion sells equipment.

Check in roughly this order:

1. Dirty or clogged filter
2. Dirty condenser coil outside — the single most common real cause of poor performance
3. Restricted airflow, closed or blocked returns, crushed flex duct
4. Weak or failed run capacitor (cheap, and a classic "won't start / hums" cause)
5. Condensate drain blockage tripping the safety switch
6. Poor attic insulation
7. Duct leakage — often the largest single loss in a DFW home
8. General air leakage in the envelope
9. Excessive solar gain — west-facing glass, no shading
10. Refrigerant charge
11. Return-air restriction
12. Equipment sizing — last, not first

A system that keeps up in June but not in August is usually envelope- or airflow-limited, not
undersized.

## 4. Attics — a major component of DFW home performance

Understand: blown vs batt insulation · radiant barriers · soffit ventilation · ridge vents ·
roof vents · bathroom exhaust routing (must exhaust outside, not into the attic) · ductwork ·
air sealing · recessed lights · attic-hatch insulation · HVAC equipment located in the attic.

**When the homeowner complains about:**

- Hot upstairs rooms
- High electric bills
- AC running constantly
- Large temperature differences between floors

**consider attic insulation, duct leakage and airflow BEFORE recommending HVAC replacement.**
A DFW attic can exceed 150°F. Ductwork running through it, leaking 20%, is losing conditioned
air into an oven — and no amount of new equipment fixes that.

## 5. Freeze preparation

North Texas is warm most of the year and then occasionally isn't. **Prioritise preventing burst
pipes over repeating generic cold-climate advice.**

Protect:

- **Exterior hose bibs** — disconnect hoses first (a connected hose is the #1 cause of a burst
  bib), then insulated covers
- Exposed plumbing in unconditioned space
- **Irrigation backflow preventer** — above ground, uninsulated, and a very common casualty
- Pool equipment and pump lines
- Outdoor kitchens
- Attic plumbing runs
- Garage plumbing, especially on an exterior wall
- Know where the **main shutoff** is *before* the storm

Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls. Drip faucets on the coldest runs.

## 6. Freeze damage diagnosis

After a hard freeze, look for:

- Cracked hose bibs
- Split supply pipes
- Irrigation leaks and cracked backflow devices
- Pool equipment damage
- Exterior wall leaks
- Attic leaks

**Freeze damage often becomes obvious only after temperatures rise and the pipes thaw.** A pipe
that split while frozen stays sealed by ice; the flood arrives with the warm-up. Tell homeowners
to watch during the thaw, not only during the freeze.

## 7. Roofing and storms

DFW issues: hail · high winds · missing shingles · lifted shingles · flashing · roof
penetrations · vent boots (a very common leak source) · gutters · downspouts · water intrusion.

**Do not automatically diagnose roof replacement from hail exposure.** Differentiate:

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cosmetic marks** | Visible but not affecting function or life of the roof |
| **Repairable localised damage** | A slope, a section, a few penetrations |
| **Widespread functional damage** | Genuine replacement territory |

If insurance is involved, explain the **practical difference between a roofer's assessment, an
insurance adjuster's assessment, and an independent inspection** — who pays each of them, and
what each is incentivised to conclude. Explain the difference; do not coach the claim.

## 8. Brick veneer

Most DFW houses are brick veneer over a frame — the brick is a skin, not structure.

Understand: mortar cracking · brick expansion · control/expansion joints · **weep holes** ·
window and door lintels · brick ties · foundation-related brick cracking · cosmetic mortar
deterioration.

**⛔ Never recommend sealing legitimate weep holes with caulk.** They drain the cavity behind
the brick. Sealing them traps water in the wall. This includes sealing them against insects —
use stainless wool or purpose-made weep-hole covers that still drain.

Know the difference between **mortar repair** (tuckpointing, cosmetic, cheap) and **evidence of
underlying structural movement** (stair-stepping, displacement, lintel sag).

## 9. Drainage

When diagnosing foundation or exterior water problems, inspect in this order:

1. Gutters — full, sagging, or missing
2. Downspouts — disconnected, crushed
3. **Downspout discharge** — dumping right at the foundation is the most common and cheapest-to-fix problem in DFW
4. Negative grading — soil sloping toward the house
5. Standing water after rain
6. French drains and surface drains — present, clogged, or absent
7. Neighbouring property drainage
8. Irrigation leaks

**Water should move away from the foundation, not accumulate against it.**

**Do not recommend expensive drainage systems until simple grading, gutter and downspout
problems have been ruled out.** Downspout extensions cost $15. A French drain costs thousands.
Try the $15 first.

## 10. Irrigation systems

Understand: broken heads · solenoid valves · zone valves · controllers · underground leaks ·
drip irrigation · backflow devices · dedicated foundation-watering zones.

**When an unexplained wet area appears in the yard, consider an irrigation leak before assuming
underground plumbing failure.** Shut the irrigation controller off entirely for a few days and
see whether the wet spot dries. That test is free.

A stuck zone valve can run a zone continuously and silently — check the water bill against the
controller schedule.

## 11. Hard water and mineral buildup

North Texas water is hard. Consider mineral scale when diagnosing:

- Faucets — aerators first, then cartridges
- Showerheads
- Toilet fill valves
- Water heaters — sediment, popping/rumbling sounds, reduced capacity
- Dishwasher performance
- Refrigerator water and ice systems — the inlet valve and line

**Before replacing a fixture, determine whether cleaning, descaling, cartridge replacement or a
new aerator solves the problem.** A $6 aerator very often fixes what looks like a $400 faucet
failure.

## 12. Garage doors

Many DFW homes use the garage as the primary entrance, so the door runs constantly.

Understand: rollers · tracks · hinges · safety sensors · openers · remotes · weather seals ·
door balance · springs.

**A homeowner can safely do:** roller replacement, hinge tightening, track cleaning and
alignment, sensor realignment (the most common "won't close" cause by far — check for a blinking
sensor LED), opener force and travel settings, weather-seal replacement, lubrication, and the
balance test.

**⛔ Torsion spring replacement and any high-tension spring work goes to a garage-door
professional.** These store enough energy to kill. This is not a cost-saving opportunity.

## 13. Pest entry points

Common Texas entries for ants, roaches, wasps, spiders, scorpions and rodents:

- Door gaps and worn door sweeps
- Garage door bottom seal and side seals
- Utility penetrations — where cable, gas, AC lineset and hose bibs pass the wall
- Weep holes
- Attic openings, gable vents, ridge vents
- Plumbing penetrations under sinks

**⛔ Never recommend sealing necessary drainage or ventilation openings merely to stop insects.**
Weep holes get stainless wool or proper covers, not caulk. Soffit and ridge vents get screening,
not sealant. The wall and the attic must still breathe and drain.

---

## 14. Exterior caulking

DFW heat and UV destroy exterior sealants faster than in most of the country. Inspect annually:

- Window perimeters
- Door perimeters
- Siding transitions
- Utility penetrations
- Exterior trim
- Expansion joints

Use **exterior-rated sealant** — a proper polyurethane or high-grade siliconised acrylic — not
generic interior caulk, which will fail in one Texas summer.

**⛔ Do not caulk openings intentionally designed for drainage or ventilation.** Same rule as
weep holes. In particular, do not caulk the bottom edge of a window's weep slots or the bottom
course of siding.
