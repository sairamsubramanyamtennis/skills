# Permits, Licensed Trades & HOA

---

## 1. The governing principle

**A handyman should not automatically perform every residential trade.** When work involves:

- Electrical systems
- Plumbing systems
- HVAC systems
- Gas
- Structural modifications
- Major remodelling

determine whether a **licensed contractor** and/or a **city permit** is required *before*
telling the homeowner how to proceed.

**⛔ Never tell the homeowner to skip a legally required permit simply to save money.** Not
directly, not as a hint, not as "most people don't bother." Unpermitted work surfaces at resale,
at the insurance claim, and at the injury — and the homeowner, not the contractor, carries it.

---

## 2. The "check current rules" discipline — this is a hard rule

**Permit requirements vary between adjacent DFW municipalities and they change.** Do not
hard-code them and do not assert them from memory.

Documented examples of the variation:

- **Plano** maintains its own residential permitting system and requirements
  — https://www.plano.gov/residential-permits
- **Prosper** explicitly lists homeowner permit requirements including items like EV charging
  circuits, water-heater replacements and HVAC replacements
  — https://prospertx.gov/718/Homeowner-Permit-Requirements

Two neighbouring North Texas cities, two different lists. That is the norm, not the exception.

### Procedure when a permit question materially affects the answer

1. **Identify the city.** Not "DFW" — the actual municipality. A Plano address and a Frisco
   address are different jurisdictions.
2. **Verify the current requirement** with a web check against the city's own site. **2–3
   searches is plenty**; do not burn a research budget on this.
3. **If you cannot verify it**, say so plainly and tell the homeowner **exactly what to ask** —
   e.g. *"Call Plano Building Inspections and ask: does replacing a 50-gallon gas water heater
   in the same location require a permit, and can a homeowner pull it themselves?"*
4. **Note whether a homeowner can pull the permit themselves.** Many Texas cities allow a
   homeowner to pull a permit for their own owner-occupied residence, which changes the cost
   calculation substantially — but this is city-specific and often limited by trade.

### Work that commonly triggers a permit in DFW cities

Treat this as a **prompt to check**, not as a statement of fact for any given city: water-heater
replacement · HVAC equipment replacement · electrical panel or service changes · new circuits
including EV chargers · re-roofing · window replacement where the opening changes · fences over a
given height · sheds over a given size · decks and pergolas · pools and pool equipment · any
structural or load-bearing change · gas line work · adding or moving plumbing fixtures ·
irrigation system installation · driveway or approach work.

### Work that generally does not

Like-for-like cosmetic and maintenance work: painting, flooring, drywall patching, faucet and
fixture swaps in the same location, garbage disposal replacement, ceiling fan swaps on an
existing box, cabinet hardware, trim, weather sealing, gutter cleaning, minor irrigation head
repair. **Still verify if the homeowner is unsure or the value is significant.**

## 3. Licensed trades in Texas

Some work is licence-gated regardless of permits. In Texas, **plumbing, electrical and HVAC** are
state-licensed trades with their own regulators, and gas work in particular is not a
handyman-scope activity.

**Never walk a homeowner through:** gas line work of any kind, electrical panel or service-entrance
work, refrigerant handling, or structural modification.

**Reasonable homeowner scope:** fixture swaps, device replacement on existing circuits where the
homeowner is competent and the breaker is off, drain clearing, appliance installation, and the
whole of the maintenance and diagnostic list in `diagnostic-playbook.md`.

**When the answer is "this needs a licensed trade," say so early in the response** rather than
after a long DIY walkthrough. Leading with a procedure and then adding "but hire a pro" at the
end trains the reader to skip the caveat.

## 4. HOA approval — a separate question entirely

DFW neighbourhoods very frequently have HOAs, and North Texas HOAs are typically active.

**For any visible exterior change**, remind the homeowner that HOA approval is its own process:

- Fences — height, material, style, stain colour, which side faces out
- Pergolas and patio covers
- Exterior paint colour
- Sheds and outbuildings
- Roofing changes — shingle colour and profile
- Solar installations
- Major landscaping and tree removal
- Exterior structures generally
- Satellite dishes, exterior lighting, storm doors, front-door colour

**⛔ Never assume "city permit approved = HOA approved."** They are entirely separate
authorities with separate processes, separate timelines, and separate criteria. A homeowner can
hold a valid city permit and still be required by the HOA to tear the work out.

**The practical sequence:** HOA architectural approval first (it is usually slower and can veto
the design), then the city permit, then the work. Doing it the other way round means the permit
may have to be amended if the HOA requires a design change.

Texas HOAs are also subject to state-law limits on what they can prohibit (solar and flags,
among others), but those limits are specific and change — **if it becomes the crux of a
question, verify the current statute rather than asserting it.**
