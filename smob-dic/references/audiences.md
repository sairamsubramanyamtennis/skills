# Audience Adaptation

Read the room first. If you have enough context, assume and begin — don't interrogate.

## Children ~6–10

**Use:** simple explanations · funny examples · animals · adventures · school situations · riddles ·
visualization · acting · guessing · short rounds · frequent encouragement.

**Avoid:** long definitions, etymology lectures, anything that feels like homework.

> **GIGANTIC**
> "Is an elephant gigantic?" — "Yes!"
> "Is an ant gigantic?" — "Only if you're another ant!"

**Best games:** Charades · Pictionary · Guess the Word · Story Chain · Association Chain.

---

## Tweens ~11–13

**Use:** mysteries · competition · school scenarios · sports · gaming · funny situations · word
battles · team challenges. Introduce roots and etymology *lightly* — as a cheat code, not a history
lesson.

**Best games:** Root Detective · Word Impostor · Which Word Doesn't Exist · Vocabulary Auction.

---

## Teenagers ~14–18

**Do not sound like a textbook.** Use music, social media, relationships, sports, movies,
technology, school, college, humor, slang vs. formal English, debate, clever insults and
compliments — words that make communication *sharper*.

> Which lands harder?
> "That argument is **bad**."
> "That argument is **incoherent**."

Then dig into why — the second one names the failure.

**Best games:** Word Court · Taboo · Vocabulary Battle · Impossible Choice · Escape Room.

---

## College Students

**Emphasize:** academic vocabulary · interviews · presentations · persuasive speaking · writing ·
debate · professional communication · precision.

Teach the difference between **sounding intelligent** and **communicating intelligently**. Never
encourage unnecessarily complicated language — an admissions essay stuffed with thesaurus words
reads worse, not better.

**Best games:** One-Minute Word Master · Root Detective (exam leverage) · Synonym Ladder · Word Court.

---

## Adults

Connect vocabulary to real life: work · travel · news · relationships · negotiations · leadership ·
humor · books · movies · everyday conversation. Bias hard toward **words they will actually use
this week.**

**Best games:** Jeopardy · Etymology Mystery · Two Truths and a Lie · Word Court.

---

## Professionals and Executives

**Focus:** precision · persuasion · leadership language · negotiation · executive communication ·
diplomacy · presentations · strategic vocabulary.

> **problem → obstacle → constraint → impediment → systemic bottleneck**

Explain the subtle differences and when each is the honest choice. Teach them to pick the *most
accurate* word, not the fanciest. Corporate rounds that work: Say It Better · Buzzword Detector ·
Diplomatic Translation · Executive Precision.

---

## Seniors

**Use:** stories · history · literature · proverbs · travel · nostalgia · famous expressions · word
origins · gentle competition · memory challenges. Prioritize social engagement and enjoyment over
scoring.

**Best games:** Jeopardy · Etymology Mystery · Word Association Chain · Generational Word Battle.

---

## Mixed-Age Groups

The hard case, and the most rewarding. **Never design a session where the oldest or most educated
participant automatically dominates.**

Rotate across several kinds of intelligence:

vocabulary · logic · popular culture · history · creativity · speed · word origins · storytelling ·
context clues

A 14-year-old should occasionally know something a 60-year-old doesn't, and vice versa. Engineer
those moments deliberately — **cross-generational discovery** is the whole point.

**Levers that equalize:**
- Team rounds pairing generations rather than pitting them
- Speed rounds (favor the young) alternating with history rounds (favor the experienced)
- Bidding and wagering games, where confidence calibration matters more than raw knowledge
- Generational Word Battle, where every participant is the expert on their own register
